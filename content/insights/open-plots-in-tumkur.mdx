Tumkur has a genuine structural advantage: it sits on the Bengaluru–Pune corridor along
NH-48, roughly 70 km from Bengaluru, with an established industrial base and a university
presence. That combination — highway access, employment, and education — is what turns
agricultural land into residential demand over a decade.

But proximity to a corridor is not by itself a reason to buy. Plots a few hundred metres
apart can behave completely differently over ten years. Four things explain most of that
difference.

## 1. Documentation, before anything else

A plot's paperwork determines whether you can ever sell it cleanly. Before price is even
worth discussing, establish:

- **Title flow** — an unbroken chain of ownership, not just the current deed.
- **Conversion status** — whether agricultural land has been formally converted for
  non-agricultural use.
- **Encumbrance certificate** — covering a meaningful period, not the minimum.
- **Approvals** — the layout's sanction from the relevant planning authority.
- **Khata and tax receipts** — current, and in the seller's name.

A plot with an attractive price and unresolved documentation is not a bargain. It is an
illiquid asset with a discount attached, and the discount usually reflects the risk
accurately.

## 2. Access that already exists

Approved road width, and whether the approach road is actually built, matters more than a
masterplan showing what it will become. Walk the approach in monsoon if you can. A site
that is hard to reach for four months a year is hard to sell for twelve.

## 3. What is being built nearby

Value follows employment and civic infrastructure, not announcements. Look for what is
already under construction within a few kilometres — industrial units, schools, hospitals,
water supply. Existing construction is evidence; a proposal is a hypothesis.

## 4. Whether the layout was planned or merely subdivided

A planned layout accounts for drainage, setbacks, plot orientation and common space. A
subdivision draws lines on a survey map. The first holds its character as it fills in; the
second tends to degrade as each owner solves their own drainage problem.

> The plots that appreciate are rarely the cheapest at purchase. They are the ones whose
> documentation, access and planning still stand up ten years later.
